UX review · Brookhaven RP · 4 November 2026

Help players turn a roleplay idea into action, and keep the freedom that makes Brookhaven work.

This is a documented UX review of Brookhaven. It's built from a recorded desktop playtest and desk research. Every claim is labelled as something I observed, a hypothesis, a proposal, or an open question. The goal is to show how I'd work on a live game this size: specific evidence first, then a cheap way to learn more, then a small change we can measure.

Summary

Three points to start with, and one open question.

The evidence supports looking into specific clarity and layout problems. It does not show that Brookhaven needs a full interface redesign or a mandatory tutorial, and I wouldn't lead with either.

Keep play visible

A house-party invitation sits on top of the house browser and covers part of its left arrow. Check the overlap across layouts, then set rules for how notifications and active tasks share the screen.

Make actions predictable

Saving a house and editing your RP name both use thumbs icons with no action words. Test whether clear verbs like Save, Cancel and Apply make players more confident.

Let players see their choices

Near a doorway, the avatar preview was badly blocked. In the open yard it was fine. Reproduce the condition before naming a technical cause.

The open question about the world: Brookhaven uses both world cues and menus. The doorbell shows an E Ring prompt and empty plots have a sign you can use. Can players reliably tell usable objects from decoration before trial and error? I didn't test furniture and fixtures well enough to answer that, so it's a research priority, not a finding.

01 · CONTEXT

What the game is, and where it's investing.

Brookhaven is a free town sandbox with no win condition. The product is the roleplay that players create together. So the UX goal isn't help me beat it. It's help me get into a story with someone quickly, and make it look the way I imagined.

The game is huge: around 80 billion visits, a peak of about 1.29 million players at once in December 2025, and weekly Friday updates. At that size a small change in first-session success touches millions of players, and a careless change does too.

Its competitors are going after the same need from different directions. Berry Avenue leans on careers and outfits. LifeTogether has a phone-style hub and an in-game social feed.

The 2025–26 changelog, read as strategy

  • Social: Family, Emote Sync, a Party system with invites and teleport to the host's house, server-wide music and announcements, expanded quick chat.
  • Expression: underglow, vehicle wraps, outfit givers, an Avatar Editor with UGC bodies and Avatar Codes, prop texture, colour and scale editing.
  • Private servers: themes, a new panel, prop saving.

The team is investing in who you play with and how you look. My proposals build on those bets rather than working against them.

02 · METHOD AND SCOPE

What I did, and what this review can and can't support.

I directed an agent-assisted walkthrough on a Windows desktop, in a public server, using an existing Roblox account, and reviewed every pass. The six recordings cover arrival, plot browsing, creating a house, spawning a vehicle, the avatar editor, tools, animations, jobs, Family, house saving and the Roblox Help screen. They total about 31 minutes, including menu inspection and recovering from failed inputs, so they don't measure how long tasks take a player.

I then compared the playtest against my desk research: changelogs, competitor coverage, parent and safety guides, and the Roblox Creator documentation.

Outside the scope

  • No mobile or gamepad testing, no participant study, no accessibility compliance audit.
  • No access to code or internal analytics. No purchases. The house save was cancelled.
  • The account wasn't fresh, so the first-time experience is described but isn't a verified new-player cohort.
  • Movement and some inputs were unreliable under automation. I didn't count those as Brookhaven bugs.
  • Other players' names and avatars are covered in every screenshot. The raw recordings stay private.

03 · THE FIRST MINUTES

Arrival introduces the tools. Starting a story is the next step.

Observed The entry sequence works as an introduction: clear Play button, a "what's new" panel, and labels on the main controls. My desk-research guess that weekly updates are hard to discover mostly didn't hold. The welcome screen covers it.

Hypothesis A new player can see that many things are possible without knowing which action starts their story. The labels name tools ("Vehicle", "House"), but players think in goals ("I want to be a doctor", "I want a family").

Proposed Test light, optional prompts based on what the player wants to do, such as Get a home, Choose a role and Explore town. They should be dismissable straight away, with the normal controls always there. The aim is to help a player act on something they already want, not to push everyone through the same quest.

Boundary This session doesn't show that the current entry flow hurts retention. Measure task completion and comprehension before proposing a bigger onboarding investment.

04 · FINDINGS

Eight findings: six to investigate, two strengths to keep.

For each one: what I saw, why it matters to a player, the next step to learn more, and a candidate change with a way to check it worked. Confidence means how strong the evidence is, not how big the problem is.

Finding 1 · Customization

The avatar preview can be blocked near a doorway

Observed
Opened near a doorway, the editor showed an extreme close-up that filled the preview. After moving into the yard and reopening it, the character was clear again.
Why it matters
Customizing depends on seeing the result of a choice. If you can't read the preview, you can select items without knowing what they'll look like. We didn't measure whether players abandon or mis-select because of it.
Next step
Reproduce it across avatar sizes, positions near doors and walls, camera distances, house types and devices. Find out whether the preview uses the world camera, collides with nearby walls, or keeps a stale camera state. These are possible causes, not conclusions.
Candidate fix
Compare a dedicated, reliably framed preview scene with a smaller fix that detects the obstruction and reframes. Done when: face, body and the selected change are readable at every tested position, and returning to play is predictable with no disorienting camera jump.
Confidence: high for the two statesReach: unknownTreat as: reliability fix

Finding 2 · Layout

Notifications overlap active navigation

Observed
The invitation stays on screen over the house browser, covering part of its left arrow. The same card stays visible across the job picker, vehicle catalog, profile editor and house saves (S04 to S11). This proves the overlap on this desktop layout. It doesn't prove clicks are blocked or that it happens on every device.
Why it matters
Invitations are valuable in a social roleplay game. The goal is to protect the task the player is doing while keeping the invite easy to find, not to hide social play.
Candidate fix
Set placement and priority rules for notifications when a panel is open. Compare a compact card, reserved space, and holding the invite until the player finishes a critical selection. Always keep a way to get back to the invite. Test touch and controller focus as well as the mouse.
Done when
No actionable notification overlaps critical browser controls on any supported layout, navigation works with every input method, and players still notice and accept invitations at an acceptable rate. Check against the engineering hitboxes, not the screenshot.
Confidence: high for the overlapReach: likely broad, unverifiedTreat as: experiment B

Finding 3 · Action language

The same icons mean different actions

Observed
House Saves uses clear slot labels, then asks "Save house and props?" with thumbs up and down. RP Name and Bio have small icons beside the fields. Doorbell notifications use thumbs too (S10). Party invites, by contrast, say Decline and Teleport. So one family of icons stands for several different actions.
Hypothesis
The surrounding text may make this clear to experienced players. Test it rather than assume it's confusing.
Candidate fix
Use explicit verbs: Save and Cancel on the confirmation, Apply name and Apply bio on the profile. First confirm with engineering when edits actually apply (straight away, on the icon, or on close). Never label something Save if it does something else. Keep the clear words that already work: Save to slot, Empty, Decline, Teleport.
How to check
Ask players to predict each result before they act, then explain whether their change stuck. Compare correct predictions, accidental commits, reversals and confidence. Pair the text with a distinct symbol so the meaning doesn't rely on colour.
Confidence: high for the patternImpact: to be testedTreat as: experiment A

Finding 4 · Discoverability

Some controls are hidden past the first toolbar

Observed
The main vertical toolbar holds avatar, tools, animations, vehicle and house. A small chevron opens a second column with an identity card and a briefcase that leads to the labelled job picker. The first-run labels cover the main column only and don't stay.
Hypothesis
A player thinking "I want to be a doctor" has to link that goal to a briefcase icon, and may first need to find the extra column. Collapsing the column keeps the world view clear and suits experienced players' habits, so there's a trade-off.
Options to compare
A labelled or more obvious expand control with clear open and closed states. Optional labels for newer players that work on touch and gamepad, not hover-only. A goal-based Choose a role entry point alongside the existing quick toolbar.
How to check
Start outside the picker and measure whether players find the path unaided, what they try first, which wrong panels they open, and how long until they've picked a role. Run a returning-player task too, so the change doesn't add steps for experienced players.
Confidence: high for structureImpact: hypothesis

Finding 5 · Catalogs

It's hard to tell what an item is and whether you can have it

What works
Scrolling, category filters with clear active states, and switching to Tools that replaces the catalog instead of stacking a second one. Keep these.
What needs research
Items are mostly thumbnails with small badges. This session couldn't find a legend for the badges, the access rules or prices. Similar items can be hard to tell apart without a name. Hover, selected category, owned, available and equipped should each look different.
Options
A short name and availability line, an optional detail preview, and a clear message about access before the player commits. Search only if real search needs justify keeping names and synonyms up to date in every language.
How to check
Did players pick the item they meant, predict whether they could have it, and recover from a wrong pick? Faster browsing isn't a win if confidence or variety drops. This is also where my desk-research question on clearly separating free and Premium items belongs. A Premium offer appeared, but I couldn't confirm what triggered it.
Confidence: moderateLinks to: trust in monetization

Finding 6 · Homes

Owning and saving a home mixes several mental models

Observed
The house browser separates Owned and Vacant and offers Go. After creating a home, a Controls House #4 button appeared. The journey crosses a physical plot, a browser, travel, choosing a house, ownership controls, customizing, and saving house and props. They're related but separate states.
Compared with my desk research
Guides disagreed on how you claim a house and warned that saves are manual. The playtest confirms saving is a deliberate step into slots, not an autosave. That's a valid design, and worth checking against what players expect to persist.
Next step
Ask players to explain where I am, what I own, what I've selected and what will persist. Check the transitions between those states, including cancelling and returning to play. A focused audit of the home journey is more useful than treating each screen as its own task.
Done when
Ownership and persistence are clearly confirmed, Back and Close behave predictably, and browsing a house is clearly different from committing a change. Check server-side state and loading feedback with engineering.
Confidence: moderateImpact: protects player trust

Finding 7 · Strength to keep

Prop state and expression give a strong base

Verified sequence
Picking the clipboard in Tools added a checkmark and a numbered hotbar slot. Equipping it added a blue outline and put it in the character's hands. Closing the catalog kept it equipped. Picking it again removed it. Animations use clear names, and the avatar editor has a live preview, saved outfits and community outfits with popularity counts.
Why keep it
Selection, ownership and what the character is holding are tied together by several visible signals. Players can check the state without reopening the catalog, and undoing it is clear. This is a much better base to refine than a blanket verdict that icon-based UI doesn't work.
Next refinement
Check whether players understand the difference between select, equip, use and remove, and track successful use rather than counting a catalog click as done. Keep the existing feedback, and make sure it works on touch and gamepad.
Confidence: highTreat as: pattern to keep

Finding 8 · Social roleplay

Picking a role isn't the same as playing one

Observed
Choosing Hospital showed selection feedback, put a role icon over the character, and added a Job button with Take Break and Quit. Family opens My Family with Create a Family.
Unresolved
What Take Break and Quit do. Joining, inviting and actually roleplaying as a group weren't tested, and neither was finding strangers to play with. My desk research flagged that as the core social problem.
Research implication
Ask a new player to act out a story idea, then watch whether the matching clothes, prop, place, action and people are easy to find. Don't treat a menu selection or a sent invite as proof that real roleplay happened.
Direction to test
Lightweight, preset-only roleplay status ("Looking for family", "On duty", "Just driving") and a "join a scene near me" list. Both build on the Party system already shipped. Check them against reports and chat-safety guardrails.
Confidence: moderateOpportunity: high

07 · ORDER OF WORK

Start with confidence and reversibility, then update with real data.

This is a framework for deciding, not a ranked backlog. I don't know the reach, effort or what the team already has in flight, and I won't make up percentages to produce a precise-looking score.

Work itemWhy look at it nowDecision gate
Invitation overlapA visible layout conflict at a known controlReproduce across layouts and confirm the real input impact
Avatar preview framingSeeing your choice was visibly blocked in one contextReproduce, then estimate how many situations and devices it affects
Explicit action verbsThe same icon pattern repeats across save and edit flowsA comprehension test shows real errors or uncertainty
Finding roles and catalog itemsLikely friction between what players want and where to startUnaided tasks and funnel data show a real barrier
World interaction cuesThe central open question, with little coverage so farAudit the objects and watch real players first

Trade-offs I'd raise with the team

  • Permanent labels help recognition but take up world space. Compare optional and context-based versions.
  • Holding back an invite protects the current task but can slow down social connection. Keep a way back to it and measure both.
  • Stable avatar framing makes choices easier to judge but removes world context. Test the least disruptive option that's reliable.
  • More world prompts help discovery but can spoil exploration and clutter the scene. Use them selectively.

How I'd avoid a superficial scoring exercise

Ask which player goal is blocked, how often it happens, who it affects, how strong the evidence is, and what it costs to learn more or change it. A small issue that reaches many players can outrank a dramatic edge case. A severe blocker can justify investigating before reach is known. Agree on the launch decision and the rollback condition before building anything.

08 · PLAYER RESEARCH PLAN

A focused round that answers the open questions.

Stage 1Establish player goals

Review onboarding and retention segments, past research, support themes and community feedback with the team. Which conventions are meant to be learned from other players? What does healthy play look like?

Stage 2Run 6 to 8 sessions

Mix new and experienced players across input types, with a verified new account for the first-time sample. This round is exploratory: it finds the mechanisms, it doesn't estimate how common they are.

Stage 3Turn it into decisions

Code each session by task, first route, interpretation, confusion, help needed, result and recovery. Write each change as a statement that could be proven wrong, and say what evidence would keep the current design.

09 · MEASUREMENT

Measure completion, not clicks.

These instruments are proposals; I haven't seen Brookhaven's analytics. Roblox funnels separate one-time onboarding from recurring flows. Any events should go into the existing schema, not a competing one.

OutcomeProposed definitionCaveat
Unaided task successIntended task completed without a moderator hint ÷ attemptsDefine where tasks and attempts start and end before testing
First-action comprehensionCorrect prediction before acting ÷ predictions collectedA research measure. Click logs can't capture it
Time to first meaningful activityFrom spawning with control to a predefined goal being reachedAccept several valid goals; don't force one path
Catalog to successful useSuccessful use ÷ eligible catalog sessionsSelecting and equipping are only intermediate states
Recovery successBack to the intended state after a wrong choice ÷ attemptsSeparate deliberate exploration from mistakes
Return behaviourD1 or D7 return for a comparable new-player cohortA later outcome; allow for acquisition and release effects

Proposed event names

ui_panel_opened with panel and entry point · catalog_item_selected with category and access state · prop_equipped / prop_removed · world_prompt_shown / world_action_completed with object class and action · house_save_confirmed / house_save_cancelled · notification_shown with type and active panel.

Data hygiene

  • Stable IDs and an attempt ID for repeatable tasks. Input type, layout bucket, version, and new vs returning.
  • Never send RP Name, Bio, chat text or unnecessary personal data.
  • Check event order, duplicates, missing completions and the right denominators before reading a funnel.
  • A prompt being shown isn't the same as a prompt being noticed.

10 · EXPERIMENTS AND SAFE DELIVERY

Small, measurable, and easy to roll back.

Experiment AAction verbs

Hypothesis: Save and Cancel labels improve prediction and reduce accidental confirmations compared with thumbs. Prototype first, then go live if needed. Keep order, size and layout fixed so only the wording changes. Guardrails: task completion, access to Cancel, speed for returning players, fit in other languages.

Experiment BInvitation placement

Hypothesis: moving notifications out of the browser's navigation improves navigation success without hurting invite recognition. Reproduce the overlap with QA first. Guardrails: invites noticed, retrieved and answered, and later social activity. Don't push invite clicks at the expense of deliberate play.

Reliability fixPreview framing

Once reproduced, a blocked avatar preview is a defect, not an A/B test of a known broken state. Use a test matrix that gives the same result every time, a canary or staged release, camera and performance checks, and monitoring of selection and exit paths.

Experiment hygiene: agree on the unit of randomization and allow for spillover, because players share servers and teach each other. Work out sample size with the analyst from baseline rates and the smallest effect worth acting on. Set stopping, rollout and rollback rules before launch. This walkthrough doesn't justify any sample size or predicted uplift.

11 · ACCESSIBILITY AND INPUT

Questions to test, not verdicts.

Small labels, translucent panels, icon-only meaning and overlapping controls all raise questions. I didn't measure contrast, touch-target sizes or controller focus paths, so I'm not calling anything inaccessible.

DimensionReview requirementEvidence to collect
InputKeyboard and mouse, touch, gamepadSame action, a cue players understand, an exit they can reach
LayoutSmall and large screens, every supported orientationNo critical overlaps or clipped content
VisionText size, contrast, colour meaningActions understood without relying on colour
Camera and motionNearby walls and objects, zoom, reduced-motion settingsA usable preview and a stable return to play
LanguageLonger translated labels, unfamiliar termsLayout stays readable and players still predict actions correctly

Who owns what matters

Players experience the Roblox platform menus and Brookhaven's own controls as one interface, but the team can only change one of them. Each proposal here names the system that owns it and an intervention the team can realistically make.

12 · HOW I'D START

A first month, and the questions I'd bring.

Week 1Understand the evidence that already exists

Meet product, design, engineering, analytics, research, QA and community. Review player goals, the roadmap, past studies, the event schema, support themes and release history. Replay these observations together and mark what's already known or in progress.

Weeks 2–3Reduce the biggest uncertainty

Run the new and experienced player round, complete the world-object audit, and reproduce the camera and layout issues across the supported devices. Prototype one small wording or notification change. Confirm baselines and data quality.

Week 4Decide with the team

Bring the evidence, the counter-evidence, the trade-offs and a proposed order. Ship one narrowly scoped, verified fix or run the smallest useful experiment, with a monitoring plan and a review date.

Questions for the team

  • What counts as a successful first Brookhaven session, and does it change with player intent or acquisition source?
  • Which conventions are meant to be learned from other players, and where do you want explicit guidance?
  • What does current research show about new players compared with experienced roleplayers?
  • Which devices and inputs matter most for UX work right now?
  • How do you balance live updates, reliability fixes, monetization and longer-term UX?
  • How do you measure social play beyond invite clicks or session length?
  • Which of these observations are already known, intentional, or being changed?
